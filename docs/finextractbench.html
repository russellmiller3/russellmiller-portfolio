FinExtractBench: can AI copy a fund report without a single wrong number?

I tested 19 AI systems on 30 real investment-fund reports. Each had to pull 22 key figures and facts into a spreadsheet. I counted one thing above all: how often it wrote a wrong number.

The job

Investors retype the same figures by hand, every quarter.

Pension funds, university endowments, insurers and family offices put money into private funds: private equity, venture capital, private credit. Every three months each fund sends its investors a report, usually a PDF of 3 to 60 pages, and every fund lays it out differently.

The investor needs the same dozen or so figures from every report, side by side in one spreadsheet, to know what its money is worth and what it owes. Pulling those figures out of a document and into a structured table is called extraction.

Who does it

  • Investment operations analysts at the investor, often by hand.
  • Fund administrators, firms paid to keep investors' books.
  • Software vendors that sell extraction; one, Canoe Intelligence, says it processes 1.6 million documents a month.

The work is large and dull. One private-markets investor posted publicly that he logged 211 hours reading agreements, chasing capital-call notices and reconciling reports that no two managers format alike.

Why it goes wrong

The right number is usually on the page, next to several wrong ones.

  • Look-alike figures. A 57-page report showed two tables a few lines apart: what the fund paid into its investments ($817.1 million) and what investors paid into the fund ($822.7 million). Only the second is "paid-in capital". In an earlier round, 7 of 10 AIs took the wrong one.
  • Look-alike quantities. One report listed both the number of fund units investors bought and the money called from them. An AI in this test entered the units as money.
  • Several periods side by side. Tables show this quarter, last quarter and last year in adjacent columns; taking the wrong column gives a real-looking, wrong number.
  • Units and currencies. "50.7" can mean $50.7 million or 50,700 thousand; Australian or Canadian dollars can be mislabelled as US dollars.
  • Missing figures. Many reports simply do not state some figures. A careless reader, human or AI, fills the gap with something plausible.
  • Scans. Some reports arrive as pictures of pages, with no text inside the file to search.
Why AI struggles

AI reads fluently, so its wrong answers look as confident as its right ones.

A modern AI can read a whole report in seconds and finds most figures correctly. Its failures are the traps above: it picks the neighbouring figure, the wrong column, or fills a gap with a plausible number. And it does not warn you. In an earlier round, chat-style AIs rated their own wrong answers at 70 to 100 out of 100 for confidence.

So a team cannot trust the AI's own sense of certainty, and checking every figure by hand removes most of the time saved. What a buyer needs to know is simpler: does this AI ever write a wrong number, and how often?

A blank is cheap: a person sees it and fills it in. A wrong number looks like every other number, passes review, and ends up in reports and decisions. So this test counts wrong numbers first.

The three with no wrong value on all 30 reports: GPT-6 Luna (OpenAI), which also found the most items right, 262 of 267, at about 1 cent a report; Gemini 4 Hydrogen (Google), 252 of 267; and GPT-6.2 Sol (OpenAI), 248 of 267.

Two more had no wrong value but did not finish every report: Claude Sonnet 5.5 (Anthropic) and Gemini 4 Neon (Google's newest), 29 of 30 each. Claude Opus 5.5, Gemini 4 Argon, Gemini 3.5 Flash and Gemini 4 Carbon made one wrong value each. The rest made 2 to 12.

Gemini 4 Carbon was first run on purpose as a control, because it had made a mistake in an earlier round: if it had scored zero too, the reports might have been too easy to catch anyone out. It made one here, which shows the test catches errors.

How sure can we be? 30 reports is a small sample. Zero mistakes in 30 is consistent with a true mistake rate of up to about 1 report in 10 (at 95% confidence). The test separates good from bad AIs, not good from slightly better.

What each AI had to find

22 items per report

  • Who and when: fund name, manager name, the date the report covers, the manager's outlook.
  • Money promised and paid: committed capital (the total investors promised), paid-in capital (how much they have handed over), unfunded commitment (what is still owed), money called this quarter.
  • Value and cash back: net asset value (what the holdings are worth today), distributions (cash paid back, to date and this quarter), fund-level borrowing.
  • Returns: IRR (average yearly return), TVPI (total value per dollar paid in), DPI (cash returned per dollar paid in), number of companies held.
  • This quarter's events: new investments, companies sold, add-on purchases, leadership changes, new contracts, events after quarter end.

Many reports leave some of these out. Only the 267 items the 30 reports clearly state are scored. An AI that fills in an item the report never mentions has made a mistake.

How I kept it fair

The right answers were written down and locked before any AI saw a report.

  1. Reports chosen by rules. Picked from web searches by rules written in advance, so I could not choose reports that suited one AI.
  2. Answer key written first. The answer key is the list of correct answers. Each records its page and the report's exact words, checked by a program. Claude, an AI from Anthropic that was not tested, drafted it.
  3. Key locked. The key and the instructions were stamped with a digital fingerprint before any AI ran, so any later change shows. Every change since is recorded with its reason.
  4. One try each, default settings. Each AI read each PDF exactly once, at its maker's default effort setting, with the same instructions. No picking the best attempt. A call only repeats if it never reached the AI, or the network dropped it.
  5. Disagreements judged blind. Wherever an AI differed from the key, the report page decided, without knowing which answer came from the AI.

That last step mattered most. In the first round the key disagreed with GPT-6.2 Sol 16 times and with Gemini 4 Hydrogen 12 times, and judged from the pages all 28 were errors or gaps in the key. When every other AI was added, 117 more disagreements were judged the same way: the key was right 76 times, the AI 34 times, both readings were acceptable 5 times, and neither 2 times. Every correction applies to every AI.

How each AI read the reports. 11 of the 19 read the PDF file directly: the Claude, GPT-6 and Gemini models. The other 8 cannot take a PDF file, so they got OpenRouter's plain-text copy of each report, which loses tables and layout: Inkling, Inkling 2 Small, Inkling 2 Mini, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, Qwen3.6 Flash, GLM 5.3 Flash, MiniMax M3.1 Flash and Kimi K3.1 Pro. Their scores reflect that handicap.

Why some AIs read fewer than 30 reports. Ran out of room: the AI spent its whole answer allowance (32,000 words of thinking) before answering. Too large: AIs that cannot read a PDF directly get OpenRouter's text copy of it, and 2 of the reports were too large to convert. Unreadable answer: the reply was not in the required format. Each counts as that AI's one try.

Scanned reports

Many reports arrive as scans: pictures of pages, with no text inside the file. I turned 10 of the 30 into scans and ran the first round's two leaders, GPT-6.2 Sol and Gemini 4 Hydrogen, again. Both still made zero mistakes and found about as many items (Sol 72 of 81, Hydrogen 70 of 81). These were clean computer-made scans; real office scans are messier.

Two AIs checking each other

A practical setup: two AIs read each report, an item is accepted only when both give the same answer, and the rest go to a person. On these 30 reports that accepted 205 items with zero mistakes and sent 66 to a person. Its blind spot is an item both AIs miss: 5 here.

Limits

What this test does not show

  • An AI drafted the answer key. The blind review corrected it wherever a tested AI disagreed, and I am checking a sample by hand. Published finance tests usually have two people write the key separately.
  • 30 reports is small. It catches an AI that slips often, not one that slips rarely.
  • One try per AI. The same AI may do a little better or worse on another run.
  • Public, digital, English reports, up to 56 pages. One turned out to be a pension plan's request for quotes, not a report; the rules picked it, so it stayed.
  • One rule changed mid-test. The control was meant to run only if the leaders scored zero before the key was corrected. They did only after, so the control ran then, and its answers were not judged blind.
  • Default settings only. Each AI ran at its maker's default effort; a higher setting might change a result.
Check it yourself

The code and the practice answer keys are public.

Anyone can rerun the test on 32 practice reports and see exactly how answers are scored: github.com/russellmiller3/finextractbench. It downloads each report from its publisher and checks it is the same file I used.

The answer keys for the 30 test reports stay private, so future AIs can be tested on reports they cannot have seen.

Related work

Where this sits

  • FinanceBench, Islam et al. 2023 (arXiv:2311.11944): questions about public companies' filings, answered with page references. This test differs: private-fund reports, the PDF read directly, scored on wrong values.
  • "Pervasive Label Errors in Test Sets", Northcutt et al. 2021 (arXiv:2103.14749): answer keys of well-known tests contain errors, best found where AIs disagree with them, as happened here.
  • FinTagging (arXiv:2505.20650) and FinCriticalED (arXiv:2511.14998): finance tests whose keys two people wrote separately.
