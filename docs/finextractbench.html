FinExtractBench: can AI copy a fund report without a single wrong number?

I tested three AI systems on 30 real investment-fund reports. Each had to pull 22 key figures and facts into a spreadsheet. I counted one thing above all: how often it wrote a wrong number.

The problem

Investors retype the same figures by hand, every quarter.

Pension funds, university endowments and family offices put money into private funds: private equity, venture capital and similar. Every three months each fund sends them a report as a PDF, and every fund lays its report out differently.

Someone on the investor's team then retypes the important figures into a spreadsheet: how much money was promised to the fund, how much has been handed over, what the holdings are worth now, how much cash came back. An investor in 50 funds does this 200 times a year.

AI could do the copying. But here a wrong number is far worse than a gap. A gap gets noticed and filled in by a person. A wrong number looks like every other number, passes review, and ends up in reports and decisions.

So this test asks one question first: does the AI ever write a number that is wrong?

The result

Two AIs made no mistakes in 30 reports. The third made one.

0wrong values: GPT-6.2 Sol and Gemini 4 Hydrogen
1wrong value: Gemini 4 Carbon

GPT-6.2 Sol is made by OpenAI; Gemini 4 Hydrogen and Gemini 4 Carbon are made by Google. They cost 12 to 15 cents a report.

Gemini 4 Carbon was included on purpose because it had made a mistake in an earlier round. It is the control: if it had also scored zero, the reports might simply have been too easy to catch anyone out. Its one mistake shows the test can catch errors.

How sure can we be? 30 reports is a small sample. Zero mistakes in 30 is consistent with a true mistake rate of up to about 1 report in 10 (at 95% confidence). The test separates good from bad AIs, not good from slightly better.

What each AI had to find

22 items per report

  • Who and when: fund name, manager name, the date the report covers, the manager's outlook.
  • Money promised and paid: committed capital (the total investors promised), paid-in capital (how much they have handed over), unfunded commitment (what is still owed), money called this quarter.
  • Value and cash back: net asset value (what the holdings are worth today), distributions (cash paid back, to date and this quarter), fund-level borrowing.
  • Returns: IRR (average yearly return), TVPI (total value per dollar paid in), DPI (cash returned per dollar paid in), number of companies held.
  • This quarter's events: new investments, companies sold, add-on purchases, leadership changes, new contracts, events after quarter end.

Many reports leave some of these out. Only the 270 items the 30 reports actually state are scored. An AI that fills in an item the report never mentions has made a mistake.

How I kept it fair

The right answers were written down and locked before any AI saw a report.

  1. Reports chosen by rules. Picked from web searches by rules written in advance, so I could not choose reports that suited one AI.
  2. Answer key written first. The answer key is the list of correct answers. Each records its page and the report's exact words, checked by a program. Claude, an AI from Anthropic that was not tested, drafted it.
  3. Key locked. The key and the instructions were stamped with a digital fingerprint before any AI ran, so any later change shows. Every change since is recorded with its reason.
  4. One try each. Each AI read each PDF exactly once. No reruns, no picking the best attempt.
  5. Disagreements judged blind. Wherever an AI differed from the key, the report page decided, without knowing which answer came from the AI.

That last step mattered most. Before it, the key disagreed with GPT-6.2 Sol 16 times and with Gemini 4 Hydrogen 12 times. Judged from the pages, all 28 were errors or gaps in the key, not the AI. Across all 57 disagreements, the key was right 19 times, the AI 22 times, and both readings were acceptable 16 times. The corrected key applies to every AI.

Scanned reports

Many reports arrive as scans: pictures of pages, with no text inside the file. I turned 10 of the 30 into scans and ran the two leading AIs again. Both still made zero mistakes and found about as many items (Sol 72 of 81, Hydrogen 70 of 81). These were clean computer-made scans; real office scans are messier.

Two AIs checking each other

A practical setup: two AIs read each report, an item is accepted only when both give the same answer, and the rest go to a person. On these 30 reports that accepted 205 items with zero mistakes and sent 66 to a person. Its blind spot is an item both AIs miss: 5 here.

Limits

What this test does not show

  • An AI drafted the answer key. The blind review corrected it wherever a tested AI disagreed, and I am checking a sample by hand. Published finance tests usually have two people write the key separately.
  • 30 reports is small. It catches an AI that slips often, not one that slips rarely.
  • One try per AI. The same AI may do a little better or worse on another run.
  • Public, digital, English reports, up to 56 pages. One turned out to be a pension plan's request for quotes, not a report; the rules picked it, so it stayed.
  • One rule changed mid-test. The control was meant to run only if the leaders scored zero before the key was corrected. They did only after, so the control ran then, and its answers were not judged blind.
  • Only three AIs. The two leaders came from an earlier round of 32 reports.
Check it yourself

The code and the practice answer keys are public.

Anyone can rerun the test on 32 practice reports and see exactly how answers are scored: github.com/russellmiller3/finextractbench. It downloads each report from its publisher and checks it is the same file I used.

Related work

Where this sits

  • FinanceBench, Islam et al. 2023 (arXiv:2311.11944): questions about public companies' filings, answered with page references. This test differs: private-fund reports, the PDF read directly, scored on wrong values.
  • "Pervasive Label Errors in Test Sets", Northcutt et al. 2021 (arXiv:2103.14749): answer keys of well-known tests contain errors, best found where AIs disagree with them, as happened here.
  • FinTagging (arXiv:2505.20650) and FinCriticalED (arXiv:2511.14998): finance tests whose keys two people wrote separately.
